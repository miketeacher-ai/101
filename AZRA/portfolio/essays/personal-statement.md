## DRAFT v1

I learned to draw before I learned to read properly, which was convenient because the building I loved most to draw was trying to fall down.

My grandmother's apartment in Balat has a crack that runs from the kitchen window to the ceiling like a river on a map. When I was six, I traced it with my finger and asked if the house was broken. She laughed and said the house had survived worse. Then she told me about 1999 — not the date, the sound. A low roar at 3 a.m., a doorway that became sacred because it held, a neighbor's stairwell that didn't. She pointed to each wall in her apartment and rated its trustworthiness, the way other grandmothers rated grandchildren.

I started sketching the apartment because I wanted to understand her fear. First, the crack, obsessively, from every angle. Then the windowsills where the marble had been worn concave by elbows, the kitchen where three women could not pass at once, the courtyard where laundry lines choreographed a daily negotiation of space. By nine, my sketchbook had become a field guide to how a building shapes a life and how a life, stubbornly, shapes it back. The crack became a datum line in my drawings, a reminder that beauty without safety is betrayal.

That idea followed me out of Balat. Istanbul's public spaces taught me the same lesson at city scale. The bus stop where I waited for school had no shade, no seating, and a timetable that had faded to illegibility — yet thirty people negotiated it every morning with choreography that deserved an architect's attention. The neighborhood library had fluorescent lights that hummed and chairs that punished anyone who stayed longer than an hour. These were not failures of budget alone; they were failures of observation. No one had drawn the people before drawing the building.

When my art teacher introduced me to plan and section drawings, the language clicked. I could finally describe the difference between a wall that divides and a wall that shelters. I taught myself SketchUp Free at our school's aging computers, staying after hours to model the courtyard I had measured by hand. The model was terrible — walls too thick, light too flat — but it was mine, and it taught me that translating a sketch into a building requires a humility that sketching alone doesn't: you must account for gravity, for water, for the way a door swing changes a room's entire social geometry.

Last summer I stopped studying buildings and started making one. With my design workshop Tasarım ve Paylaş, twenty peers and I built a small library nook in our school courtyard. We had no budget, so we had to listen: to the vice principal who feared vandalism, to the janitor who knew where the sun actually fell at 2 p.m., to the shyest first-year who quietly suggested we make the shelves low enough for sitting. Reclaimed pallets became seating; sandpaper and two coats of paint became pride. When younger students started using the nook before we had finished cleaning up, I understood what my grandmother had been trying to teach me: a safe, beautiful space is not a luxury. It is permission to stay, to belong, to survive the next thing that shakes.

I want to study architecture because I want to earn the right to draw spaces that hold people — literally and otherwise. My portfolio holds three projects born in these streets: a 45-square-meter micro-apartment that gives a Balat family privacy without isolation, a Kadıköy bus stop that treats waiting as part of life rather than its interruption, and a pavilion designed to remain standing when everything else is tested. Behind every line is that crack in my grandmother's wall, still there, still measured, still teaching me that the most important thing a building can do is keep its promise.

She still lives there. I still draw her apartment. The crack hasn't grown in two years — I check each visit, with a pencil for scale — but I no longer trace it with fear. I trace it to remember the standard.
