## DRAFT v1 (Day 32)

Every Tuesday for three years, my mother rewrote the menu board of our shop before sunrise. Not because we changed what we sold — borek is borek — but because by Tuesday morning, yesterday's prices were lies. Flour had climbed overnight. I know because it was my job to check.

I was thirteen when she handed me her phone and asked me to make sense of a chart from the central bank. The line went up like a staircase drawn in anger. I didn't understand inflation; I understood that neighbors stopped buying cheese, and my mother started writing smaller numbers in red ink.

So I made a spreadsheet. First it tracked flour, butter, electricity vs menu prices. Then everything — exchange rates at breakfast, minimum-wage announcements, bread package weights that stayed same price while shrinking. By fifteen, my spreadsheet predicted Tuesday's chalkboard before she felt dread writing it. When the corner bakery argued he "had no choice," I showed him: costs +11% while prices +4% — he was subsidizing neighbors' breakfasts out of love, slowly going under.

The habit became a question: why did some shops survive same storm that drowned others? Answer kept pointing to things I couldn't see from counter: expectations, policy credibility, mathematics of trust. Inflation, I learned, is not about money. It's about promises — whether people believe tomorrow's lira will resemble today's.

That sent me to official data. While friends scrolled feeds, I pulled World Bank tables and taught myself Python to graph twenty years of Turkish inflation beside Poland and Mexico. Same global shocks after 2021, different recoveries — difference was institutional, not meteorological. My graphs were ugly but honest, now in a public repo with my paper on how rising prices reshaped what households bought: less meat, more flour; smaller weddings, later appliances. Economists call these adjustments. I watched them one customer at a time.

Last spring I turned spreadsheet outward. I designed five-session Money Skills workshop and taught thirty students why menu board keeps changing — real vs nominal, compounding, debt traps in installment plans. Their quiz scores rose 40%. One boy said he finally understood his father's car loan. That moment keeps echoing: economics as translation service between frightening abstractions and ordinary Tuesdays.

I want to study economics to build tools my mother needed earlier — clearer data, better defaults, policies respecting shopkeeper's Tuesday. Universities offer rigorous training; I bring harder-to-teach: memory of red ink on chalkboard, certainty that behind every chart is someone deciding what to give up.

My mother still rewrites menu every Tuesday. Inflation cooled; habit remains. Now she asks what numbers will say — and lately, they say what I calculated.
