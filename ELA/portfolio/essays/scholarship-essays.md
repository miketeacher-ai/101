## Essay 1: Leadership and Community Impact

First session of Money Skills had nine students and one crisis. Boy named Emir raised hand and asked why father's car loan — "12 equal installments, very fair" — cost nearly double sticker price. I drew amortization on board while thirty eyes watched interest compound in real time. Silence afterward was good kind: sound of mental model breaking.

Workshop series was not first community project, but first I measured. Over five weeks, thirty-plus students learned needs-versus-wants tracking, real vs nominal prices, compound growth, debt mechanics, first-income basics. I wrote curriculum from scratch, taught every session, ran pre/post quizzes, published ten posts translating inflation research into plain Turkish. Average quiz rose 40% — but numbers were never point. Point was what Emir said at graduation: "Nobody ever showed us math of our own lives."

Leadership, I've learned, is mostly logistics plus nerve. Logistics: booking rooms, recruiting volunteers, designing worksheets that work at 8am Saturdays. Nerve: teaching inflation to students who live it, without pretending teenager can fix macroeconomics — but refusing to pretend understanding is useless either.

My paper examined how Turkish households adjusted consumption during inflation episode; workshops attacked same problem from other end, giving families tools before next shock. Both grew from watching mother reprice shop menu weekly for three years, absorbing in red chalk what policy debates discuss in percentages.

I apply for full funding not as charity but as leverage. Every dollar converts directly into continued work — deeper econometrics, better curriculum, wider reach. Give me training, and Tuesday sessions keep multiplying: new cities, new volunteers, more people who can read mathematics of own lives.

## Essay 2: Financial Need and Commitment to Return

Full transparency about family finances would show small business surviving one of steepest inflation episodes in modern history — resilient, proud, unable to fund American education at any realistic discount. Parents run shop grandfather opened; savings are inventory. When I showed US tuition figures, father did calculation Turkish fathers do: house, retirement, younger sister's future. Answer is simply no — which is why every application carries same request: meet full demonstrated need.

I want precise about what full aid means to someone like me. It does not mean comfort. It means difference between studying economics and merely reading about it online between shifts. It means spreadsheet habit — started at thirteen with flour prices — gets four years rigorous training instead of guessing. It means Money Skills keeps running through volunteers I train rather than dying when I leave Ankara.

And commitment, stated plainly: I intend to bring this back. Turkey's households need financial infrastructure built by people understanding both data and counter — both World Bank tables and smell of fresh borek at dawn. Whether through central-bank research, consumer-protection policy, or fintech for small merchants like parents, my career will be measured in Turkish Tuesdays made less frightening.

Aid me and you are not funding student's escape. You are investing in translator between frightening abstractions and ordinary people — role I've already played for free, and intend to professionalize.
