Boston College asks applicants to consider what they will do for others, which is right question for me — my economics journey has never been private. It started behind family's shop counter during lira crisis, watching neighbors absorb price shocks silently, and became public work: research paper on household adjustment built from World Bank data, then Money Skills workshop series teaching thirty students why parents' budgets kept breaking.

BC fits that trajectory in three ways. Its Carroll School treats finance as form of service — kind of framing making workshop curriculum feel at home rather than outlier hobby. Its economics department offers empirical depth my self-taught pandas now begs for, with small classes where questions about inflation expectations would not get lost (verify current offerings). And formation-of-persons ethos matches how I already operate: every project shipped — paper, workshops, ten posts translating inflation into plain Turkish — aimed at usefulness first.

Jesuit tradition of reflection also names something sprint taught me: shipping fast is easy; knowing WHY you ship is discipline. My 45-day portfolio produced measurable outputs because each answered person, not admissions committee — baker going under quietly, teenager confused by father's loan terms.

I want four years of rigorous economics inside community that keeps asking who benefits. BC is that community, and Carroll is where counter knowledge becomes responsible finance.
