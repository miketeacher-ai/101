## Draft (~300 words)

My mother's bakery menu board is the most honest economic indicator I know. Every Tuesday it is rewritten, every rewrite encodes a week's worth of inflation, negotiation, and quiet panic. If I had to explain my country's economy to a visitor, I'd skip statistics and walk them past that chalkboard.

Here is what board knows. It knows flour does not rise smoothly — it lurches, so prices must be erasable chalk, never paint. It knows customers don't experience inflation as percentage; they experience day borek quietly shrinks by centimeter while price holds. It knows difference between nominal and real is not academic: it's whether regular still buys two instead of one.

When I finally studied inflation formally — pulling World Bank tables, teaching myself Python, graphing twenty years of Turkish price levels against Poland and Mexico — charts surprised me by confirming chalkboard. Headline indices smoothed what households lived as cliffs. My paper argues official baskets understate how families actually adjust: less meat before less bread, smaller weddings before fewer appliances. Board told me first.

UChicago prizes questions refusing to sit in lanes, and mine never did: started in shop, passed through spreadsheet, public dataset, GitHub repo, workshop where thirty teenagers finally understood fathers' loans. What I really ask is very Chicago question: when data and lived experience disagree, which do you trust — and can you build tools making them agree?

I intend to spend four years on that problem, starting with better chalk.
