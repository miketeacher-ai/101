## DRAFT v1

I know the exact weight of my grandmother. Not from a scale, but from how my arms felt carrying her — forty-seven kilograms, plus the bag of documents we always brought to the village clinic because the clinic never had them.

The clinic served five villages from two rooms. One doctor, when he wasn't reassigned to the city. One nurse who knew everyone's name and every cabinet's emptiness. I started accompanying my grandmother at twelve because my mother couldn't miss work and my grandmother's knees couldn't miss the bus — a two-hour ride that ended in a waiting room where time was the only abundant resource. I learned to translate between her Turkish and the doctor's hurried explanations, and between the doctor's prescriptions and her careful questions about whether the pills would interact with her blood-pressure medicine that we had bought without prescription from the town pharmacy.

What struck me was not the scarcity alone, but its shape. The doctor was excellent and exhausted. He could diagnose in minutes what mattered, then spend an hour on paperwork that a rural clinic still did on paper. He knew my grandmother's history better than any file, because files didn't travel between villages. When he finally saw her, he was brilliant. The problem was everything before and after that moment.

So I did what teenagers do when they notice a system: I tried to fix it in the wrong way first. I taught myself to organize her papers chronologically, then in a spreadsheet, then — after a school coding club introduced me to Python — in a small program that reminded her when to take each pill and flagged interactions I scraped from public drug databases. It was clumsy and broke often, but my grandmother's adherence went from approximate to consistent. More importantly, she stopped carrying the bag of documents. The clinic visit shrank from a day to two hours because we arrived with answers instead of questions.

That experience sent me in two directions that have defined my last two years. Toward biology: I wanted to understand what those pills did to those proteins, what hypertension does to those vessels. I joined my school's biology olympiad preparation, spent Saturdays on Khan Academy and free university lectures, and chose a computational biology project for this sprint — docking simulations that explore how a small molecule might inhibit a bacterial target, work I publish on GitHub with figures that regenerate in under five minutes. The code is not medicine, but it is adjacent: it explores how we might extend one lab's reach the way my pill program tried to extend one doctor's.

And toward community: I realized my grandmother was not unique. Twenty hours of shadowing at our local poliklinik, permitted after I wrote a formal request and promised to touch nothing, taught me that every waiting room holds grandmothers with bags. So I organized a one-day health fair for my town with two nursing-student volunteers: blood-pressure and BMI stations that measured 127 neighbors, education booths that explained what those numbers meant in plain Turkish, and a pre/post quiz that showed a twenty-five percent gain in understanding. The fair cost almost nothing; its most valuable supply was permission, which I had learned to ask for on Day 16 from the muhtar.

I want to study molecular biology not away from that clinic, but because of it — as the science that lets one person's care travel. My sprint paper explores proteins; my shadowing taught me patients; my health fair taught me systems. Together they form a single argument: medicine's future belongs to people fluent in both pipettes and waiting rooms.

My grandmother still carries her bag, out of habit. I still carry her, sometimes literally, but more often through code and study. She tells neighbors I will become a doctor. I tell her I want to become the kind of doctor who makes sure there is one.
