## Essay 1: Leadership and Community Health Impact

The health fair began with a question from the muhtar: "Why would healthy people come to be measured?" I had prepared a flyer about blood pressure and BMI, but his question was better — it was about motivation, not information. So I changed the flyer. Instead of "Free Health Screening," it said, "Do you know your numbers? Find out in five minutes, with tea." One hundred twenty-seven people came.

That shift — from what I wanted to teach to what they wanted to know — summarized twenty hours of shadowing that preceded the fair. At the poliklinik, I watched a doctor diagnose in minutes what paperwork consumed for an hour. I logged each hour with date, department, supervisor initials, and one thing I had observed, not assumed: how the nurse knew which cabinet was empty before opening it, how the doctor taught a mother to count pills using the days of the week rather than milligrams. Leadership, I learned, is mostly logistics plus permission plus listening.

The fair's logistics were deliberately simple: two nursing-student volunteers I had recruited from a local vocational school, a borrowed blood-pressure monitor, a BMI chart taped to a table, education booths with handouts I had written in plain Turkish after testing them on my grandmother. Pre-quiz average was 52%; post-quiz was 77% — a twenty-five percent gain that mattered less than the two anonymized quotes I collected: "I didn't know my pressure could be high without feeling anything," and "My daughter will come next time."

I apply for full funding not as charity but as leverage. Every dollar converts into continued work — deeper molecular training, better community tools, wider reach. Give me the lab, and the waiting room keeps shrinking: more fairs, more students trained, more grandmothers who don't need to carry bags.

## Essay 2: Financial Need and Commitment to Return

Full transparency about my family's finances would show a small salary, a grandmother's pension that pays for her medicines before it pays for anything else, and savings that are the difference between a bus ticket and a missed clinic appointment. My parents supported my olympiad preparation and my coding habit with time more than money — driving me to the city library for free Wi-Fi when our connection failed. American tuition figures read to us like distances to another planet.

So I state my need plainly: without full demonstrated need met, I cannot attend any US university, no matter how strong my paper or fair. My family's contribution is their blessing and my grandmother's stories, not dollars. This is why every application I submit carries the same request: meet my full need, or not at all — half solutions produce debt that a future doctor serving underserved communities cannot repay.

And here is my commitment, stated plainly because scholarship committees deserve plainness: I intend to return. Not immediately — I need the rigorous molecular training, the research mentorship, the clinical exposure that top US programs provide — but ultimately, to serve where doctors are scarce and grandmothers still carry bags. Whether through research on accessible diagnostics, public-health work on hypertension screening, or clinical practice in a system that remembers for patients, my career will be measured in waiting rooms made shorter.

Aid me and you are not funding a student's escape. You are investing in a translator between pipettes and waiting rooms — a role I have already played for free, and intend to professionalize where it is most needed: at home.
