My architecture education began in a Balat apartment where my grandmother pointed to cracks and told me about 1999 — not the date, the sound. I have been drawing buildings that hold people safely ever since, which is why WashU's College of Architecture reads less like a school and more like a continuation.

Three specifics anchor my interest. First, the studio sequence starts making real things immediately; my 45-day portfolio sprint — a Balat micro-apartment retrofit, a Kadıköy bus stop redesign, an earthquake-resilient pavilion — was my homemade version of exactly that pedagogy. Second, WashU's civic design engagement around St. Louis mirrors what I just did at home: founding Tasarım ve Paylaş for twenty-plus peers and leading a courtyard library nook build with reclaimed materials (verify current community programs). Third, Sam Fox research on resilient environments speaks directly to Turkey's seismic reality — I want to study where structural safety is a design problem, not only engineering (verify initiatives).

I would arrive with measured sketches of Istanbul streetscapes, models rebuilt twice after failures, a workshop curriculum any campus could reuse. And the question underneath all of it, from my grandmother's finger on the wall crack: how do we make ordinary buildings worthy of trust?

WashU is where I intend to answer it — with wood glue on my hands for four years.
